A History of Hurst Hall's (In)Accessibility

April 9, 2026


By Roxy Alexander

American University’s campus (pictured in Fig 1) began taking shape with the construction of Hurst Hall in 1897. Since its ceremonial unveiling, Hurst Hall (building six in fig 1) has remained largely the same. The lack of perceived action taken by American University admin to make this historic building more accessible has caused a rift between the University and many past and current disabled students. Is the university complacent in the current inaccessibility status of Hurst Hall?

On April 5th, 1999, nine years after the monumental passing of the Americans with Disabilities Act, a student and staff writer at American University published a piece in AU’s student newspaper The Eagle with the headline “Buildings remain inaccessible.” Jennifer A. Kepka’s article highlights American University’s long-standing pattern of moving people instead of fixing inaccessible buildings. She writes with a neutral attitude, ensuring that both shortcomings and plans for change are highlighted. At the time of writing this piece, Kepka was able to interview the sitting Assistant Vice President for Facilities and Administrative Services Jorge Abud on the matter. He was quoted saying “We’re required to make all of our programs accessible; that doesn’t necessarily mean all of our buildings must be accessible.”1 Abud’s primary solution to making higher education available to everyone was by moving classrooms with disabled students to buildings that were accessible. His interpretation of how requirements can be met is shared widely by university admin at the time. For instance, the Director of Disability Services, Shelley Reeves, stated just that: “One way of meeting accessibility [needs] is to relocate [students and departments] to accessible buildings.”2 While the primary solution was to move students before fixing buildings, Reeves and Abud both declared AU’s commitment to “increase accessibility around campus” and revealed that the “accessibility plans in both buildings [Hurst and SIS] [were] being considered.”3 At the time, admins “goal of making all our buildings accessible over time” brought students hope for a future with fully accessible buildings.4

It has been nearly three decades since Kepka’s article was published in The Eagle. Figure 2 is a current map (as of 2022) of accessible buildings and paths on American University’s main campus. As noted in the key at the right of the figure, there are three types of buildings on campus: dark blue buildings are barrier-free, light blue are not accessible, and striped are partially accessible. Buildings are designated as barrier-free when they have: 1) an accessible entrance, 2) accessible paths throughout the building to main functions on all floors, and 3) accessible restrooms. Buildings are considered not accessible when: 1) there is no elevator, 2) there is not an accessible entrance to the building, and 3) there are no accessible restrooms. A building’s status is partially accessible when they have: 1) an accessible entrance to the building, 2) an accessible path to main functions on the entry level floor only, or 3) the elevator is not freely available. At the time of Kepka’s article, four buildings were cited as inaccessible by Kepka (Hurst Hall, East Quad Building, Mckinley, and the Experimental theater). Since then, only two have been made barrier free. While the East Quad building remains as it was in 1999, inaccessible, Hurst Hall has gained a new classification as partially accessible. When did this change occur, what gives Hurst Hall the status of partially accessible, and is this status accurate?

In the fall of 2017, AU undergrad Bobby Zitzman wrote an article for the Eagle not too dissimilar from that of Kepka’s. However, in this article Zitzman speaks to their work on the American Undergraduate Student Government (AUSG). Specifically, Zitzman addresses a bill they introduced to the AUSG encouraging the AUSG President to advocate for structural changes to be made to Hurst Hall, East Quad, and other inaccessible buildings on campus. In 2018, Brain Fu, a student from the College of Arts and Sciences helped AUSG draft Senate Bill 16-17-024, with a section proposing better physical accessibility on campus, notifying “administrators of nine locations on campus that needed to be improved.”5 Following the 2018 bill, a lift was installed to the entrance of Hurst Hall giving it the status of partially accessible.

To be sure, the efforts of many named and unnamed student advocates has led to successful wins for the disabled community at AU, like the installation of the external lift in the oldest building on campus. Despite the success in removing entrance barriers, Hurst Hall remains non-accessible to mobility impaired students.6 For instance, in an email exchange between myself and a former AU student and founder of AU’s Disability Student Union, the following was given in response to my inquiry on Hurst Hall’s history of inaccessibility: “Due to inaccessibility, I have never been inside Hurst during my time as a student.” Despite having received both their undergraduate and graduate degree from American University, this student never physically entered Hurst Hall. Another student claimed that accessing the lift is challenging as it requires a key distributed only to students that can prove their physical impairment with medical documentation. Further, when the key is acquired, the lift itself is shaky and causes many to be fearful of use. Once within Hurst Hall access to the lower and upper floors, and restrooms is impossible due to the lack of an elevator. Therefore, AU’s designation of campus buildings as partially accessible is misleading and runs the risk of normalizing the lack of accessibility on campus.
